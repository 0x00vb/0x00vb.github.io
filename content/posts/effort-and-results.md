“Society rewards results, not effort” sounds true when you have worked hard on something nobody wanted.

It also leaves out a lot. Results depend on timing, access, health, luck, and what other people choose to notice. A market outcome is not a complete measure of a person's contribution or worth.

Still, there is a useful engineering lesson inside the harsher version of the sentence.

## The user gets the outcome

A person trying to pay an invoice does not experience the elegance of your refactor. They experience whether the page loads, whether the amount is right, and whether they can finish without worrying that they paid twice.

Your effort matters because it can improve those things. It does not obligate the user to appreciate the process independently of them.

That is a useful correction when a project starts organizing itself around how busy the team feels.

## Invisible work still counts

A tested restore procedure may produce no visible change on an ordinary day. Neither does fixing an authorization boundary before someone crosses it.

These are results too. Their value is a risk reduced or an option preserved. They are harder to observe, which makes explaining them part of the work.

Counting only immediately visible output would encourage exactly the wrong behavior.

## Give effort a direction

Before investing another week, name the change you expect. Will a task become faster? Will an error become less likely? Will someone be able to do something they currently cannot?

Then choose a way to find out. Sometimes that is a metric. Sometimes it is watching a person attempt the task. Sometimes it is a recovery exercise that exposes a missing step.

I would keep effort as a personal commitment and treat outcomes as feedback. Work carefully, then remain willing to discover that the work did not help as much as you hoped.

That is less satisfying than a slogan. It is more useful when deciding what to do tomorrow.
